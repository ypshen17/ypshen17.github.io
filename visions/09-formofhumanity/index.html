Architecture as Witness in Crisis

Tangible and Intangible Forces in Space

Architecture is not merely about constructing buildings; it is about constructing narratives, telling stories of human resilience, memory, and recovery. In times of crisis, architecture plays a crucial role, not only in providing shelter but in preserving the intangible qualities of human experience. The image of a serene church standing in contrast to the chaos of a storm captures this tension between the tangible and intangible aspects of architecture. It is a powerful reminder that spaces are never neutral; they are repositories of memory, emotion, and history.

In addressing humanitarian crises, whether caused by war, natural disaster, or social upheaval, architecture has both a practical and symbolic role. For example, Shigeru Ban’s paper tube shelters, created in the wake of earthquakes and tsunamis, provide not only shelter but a sense of hope and continuity. Similarly, the construction of emergency hospitals, such as those during the COVID-19 pandemic, demonstrates how architecture can adapt to immediate needs while reflecting our collective human values of solidarity and care.

But architecture’s role goes beyond the physical. It is not just about creating safe spaces—it is about giving form to the memory and trauma of those who have suffered. Spaces embody the scars of conflict and survival, and transform into silent archives of resilience and loss. In this sense, architecture becomes a medium through which we reflect on our shared humanity, providing not just protection but a lasting record of the forces that shape our societies.

The tension between the tangible and the intangible, the solid structures that provide refuge, and the emotions, sounds, and memories that those spaces carry, forms the core of my research. Through the lens of Sonic Urbanism, I examine how sounds and atmospheres permeate architectural forms, shaping their lived reality. These invisible forces are just as powerful as the physical structures they inhabit, and understanding this interaction is essential for creating spaces that not only respond to immediate needs but also acknowledge the deeper, long-term impacts of trauma and recovery.

In this way, architecture does more than protect. It heals, remembers, and shapes the future. Acknowledging space’s dual role as a crisis response and a memory vessel challenges us to see architecture beyond its immediate utility. By engaging with both the tangible, the physical form of spaces, and the intangible, the sounds, memories, and traumas embedded in those spaces, we can design environments that are more responsive, empathetic, and aware of the forces at play.
