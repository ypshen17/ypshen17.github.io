Something about Rice

Rice entangles species, infrastructures, and histories. Each grain embodies the precarious balance of survival, more-than-human collaboration, and political negotiation.

Rice fields are not passive landscapes; they are assemblages of mud, microbes, water buffalo, insects, and human labor. They teach us that stability is provisional, maintained through fragile alignments across species and forces. Unlike monuments of concrete or steel, the rice field’s endurance lies in its vulnerability — floods, pests, and human displacement always threaten its rhythm.

Tsing shows us that to study life in ruin is to witness unexpected forms of coexistence. Rice, however, complicates this narrative: it is not only found in ruins but also sustains empires, global economies, and everyday survival. Its cultivation reveals both the promise of shared sustenance and the violence of extraction. To imagine a “rice composition” is to see how infrastructures of care and infrastructures of control weave together. It is a reminder that urbanism, too, must be read as a field — cultivated, precarious, and never free from entanglement with nonhuman actors.

Roots in water. Worlds entangled.
